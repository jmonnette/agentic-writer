# Proposal Format Skill

## Purpose

Defines structural and persuasive conventions for client-facing proposals. Load alongside `personal-voice` for all proposal writing. The author's voice DNA (rhetorical patterns, practitioner authority, evidence-first reasoning) applies here. Only the structure and tonal calibration differ from articles.

---

## Tonal Calibration

- **Formality**: 8/10 (vs. 7/10 for articles). Professional and precise. Remove colloquialisms that work in articles ("garbage in/out," "pajama time," "vibe coding").
- **Client-centric language**: Use "your organization," "your team," "your context" throughout — not the generic "companies" or "organizations."
- **Solution-oriented**: Articles analyze; proposals prescribe. Every section advances the case for the proposed solution.
- **Committed, not hedged**: Unlike thought leadership (which acknowledges uncertainty as intellectual honesty), proposals make specific commitments. Replace "shows promise" and "might" with specific deliverables and timelines.
- **We / Our**: Use "we" for the proposing team. Establish partnership framing early and maintain it.
- **No scare quotes**: The hype-term-in-quotes move works in articles; in proposals it can undermine credibility with clients who use that terminology.
- **No em dashes**: Consistent with personal-voice rule. Use colons, commas, or periods instead.

## Standard Proposal Structure

### 1. Cover / Title Page
- Engagement name
- Client name
- Date submitted
- Proposing organization and point of contact

### 2. Executive Summary (5-10% of total)
- Problem statement in 1-2 sentences, framed from the client's perspective
- Proposed solution in 1-2 sentences
- 3 key outcomes (bullets, no more)
- Recommended next steps with a date
- **Write this section last.** It is the section most executives read.

### 3. Context / Problem Statement (10-15%)
- Articulate the client's situation as they experience it, not as an outside diagnosis.
- Demonstrate understanding: their industry, their specific challenge, constraints they have named.
- The Diagnostic Reframe from personal-voice works here: name the wrong frame the client may be operating from, then supply the right one.
- End with a crisp, one- or two-sentence problem statement.

### 4. Proposed Solution / Approach (20-25%)
- Lead with strategic framing: what kind of engagement this is and why this approach fits this problem.
- Break into phases or workstreams where applicable.
- For each phase: objective, key activities, who leads, duration.
- Be specific about methodology. Not "we will assess" but "we will conduct X stakeholder interviews and evaluate findings against Y criteria."
- Tie every element back to the problem statement from Section 3.

### 5. Scope of Work (15-20%)
- **In scope**: bulleted list, unambiguous
- **Out of scope**: explicit exclusions that prevent scope creep and set expectations
- **Assumptions**: conditions under which the scope and timeline hold
- **Dependencies**: what the client must provide for the engagement to succeed

### 6. Deliverables
- List every artifact the client will receive.
- For each: name, format (deck, report, workshop, etc.), primary audience (executive or working team), and delivery timing.
- No vague deliverables. Not "recommendations document" — name the document and describe its substance in one sentence.

### 7. Timeline / Milestones
- Table or phased visual format.
- Major milestones with dates or week numbers.
- Client review and approval gates called out explicitly.
- Build buffer into the schedule. Never publish a timeline with no slack.

### 8. Team
- Named individuals for key roles, not just role labels.
- 2-3 sentence bios focused on relevant experience for this specific engagement.
- Make the case for why this team fits this client's problem, not a general capabilities list.

### 9. Investment / Pricing
- Lead with the total investment figure before breaking it into components. Never bury the number.
- Use "investment" not "cost" or "price."
- Break down by phase or workstream to justify the total.
- Payment schedule tied explicitly to milestone delivery.
- State what is included and what would trigger a change order.

### 10. Why Us (include only when competing)
- 3-4 differentiators, not a generic capabilities paragraph.
- Specific proof points: named clients (with permission), outcomes with numbers.
- If this section cannot be made specific to this client and engagement, cut it.

### 11. Next Steps
- Clear, specific, time-bound: "To begin work by [date], we need a signed agreement by [date]."
- Name the decision-maker action required.
- Provide a single contact for questions.

## Proposal-Specific Persuasion Patterns

**Problem Mirroring**: Echo the client's exact language when describing their problem. If they said "we're stuck in pilot purgatory," use that phrase. It signals you listened.

**Stakes Escalation**: After naming the problem, quantify what continued inaction costs. Use industry benchmarks if client-specific numbers are unavailable.

**Proof-Then-Promise**: In the Approach section, don't just describe what you will do. Show you have done it before (proof), then commit to the outcome (promise).

**Assumption Transparency**: Call out every major assumption explicitly. Clients trust proposals that acknowledge uncertainty more than proposals that project false confidence.

**The "You Decide" Close**: End the Executive Summary or Next Steps with language that gives agency to the client: "You decide whether this is the right moment to move." Not a pressure tactic. Not passivity. Confident respect.

**Negation-Then-Affirmation (Proposal Variant)**: The personal-voice signature move applies in the Context section: "This isn't a technology problem. It's a change management problem." Useful for reframing before presenting your solution.

---

## Formatting Conventions

- **Tables** for: timelines, scope matrices, pricing breakdowns, team bios side-by-side
- **Bold** for: section headers, key commitments, named deliverables, and the total investment figure
- No bullet-only pages. Every major section needs at least one paragraph of prose.
- Page or section numbers for reference during review conversations.
- Consistent terminology throughout. Pick one name for each workstream, phase, and deliverable, then use it everywhere.

---

## Length Norms

| Proposal Type | Page Count | Word Count |
|---|---|---|
| Short (new client, small engagement) | 4-6 pages | 1,500-2,500 words |
| Medium (known client, multi-phase work) | 8-12 pages | 3,000-5,000 words |
| Large (RFP response, strategic engagement) | 15-25 pages | 5,000+ words (may include appendices) |

---

## What to Avoid

- ❌ Vague commitments: "we will explore," "we will look into" — state what will be delivered
- ❌ Jargon without definition (assume client may not share your internal vocabulary)
- ❌ Credentials section before the problem section (show you understand before you sell yourself)
- ❌ Open-ended scope language: "and anything else needed" — scope is always closed
- ❌ Generic copy-pasted boilerplate from another proposal
- ❌ Scare quotes around client-facing terminology
- ❌ Em dashes
- ❌ Burying the total investment figure at the end of the pricing breakdown

---

## Quality Checklist (Proposal-Specific)

Before delivering to client:
- [ ] Executive summary written last and reads as a standalone document
- [ ] Problem statement mirrors the client's own language where possible
- [ ] Every deliverable is named specifically with format and audience
- [ ] Scope has explicit In Scope / Out of Scope / Assumptions sections
- [ ] Timeline includes client gates and visible buffer
- [ ] Total investment figure is visible up front in the pricing section
- [ ] Team section names actual people with proof points relevant to this engagement
- [ ] Next Steps section includes a specific date and named action
- [ ] No vague commitments remain
- [ ] No orphaned statistics (personal-voice rule: every data point gets immediate interpretation)
- [ ] Consistent terminology throughout (one name per deliverable/phase)
- [ ] No em dashes
